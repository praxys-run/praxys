## Question and product purpose

For nonclinical adults with an existing running base, including sparse device history and no gym, what supports executable daily, first-finish and performance preparation modules through single-day 50,000 m goals, and which exact long-duration, descent and full-cycle prescriptions remain unsupported?

Provide a successor review package for Issue #798 with positive executable candidates across the ten requested research domains, exact guardrails, explicit partial support and immutable history. This is not evidence or prescription acceptance.

## Scope

## Population

- Adults aged at least 18 with an established running base; nonclinical daily, first-trail/first-distance finish or performance goals
- Different resource access and strength experience, including no gym and sparse device history

## Intervention or exposure

- General running reuse, structured history bridge, eccentric-control/home strength, positive-incline treadmill and stairs
- Concurrent running and strength, run/walk, fuel practice, taper, feedback and non-medical restart

## Comparator

- Running-only or conventional resistance programmes
- Different equipment, session placement, continuous running, pre-taper training and cessation

## Outcomes

- Muscle function, running performance, fatigue and soreness, tolerance and practical applicability
- Uncertainty in automated doses, terrain transfer and complete first-50-km preparation

## Claims

### `trail-program.base-transfer-is-partial` — moderate

Aerobic capacity and other conventional endurance characteristics relate to trail performance, so established general running can support a basic module. Trail performance is multifactorial; road fitness does not establish descent skill, comparable exposure or a finish forecast.

- **Sources:** `de-waal-2021-performance-review`, `van-hooren-2020-treadmill-biomechanics`
- **Population:** Adult runners in heterogeneous trail studies and acute treadmill/overground comparisons
- **Domain:** General running reuse; Daily training
- **Limitations:**
  - The trail review includes seven studies and correlational models, not a validated automated prescription.
  - Treadmill comparisons are acute and mainly level; no universal grade or outdoor-equivalence conversion follows.

### `trail-program.sparse-records-need-provenance` — low

Running history and changes in exposure are relevant to planning; absent device data cannot be equated with a novice biological state. A structured athlete-reported bridge is an explicit Praxys option, not a validated evidence-equivalence or safety classifier.

- **Sources:** `frandsen-2025`, `buist-2008`
- **Population:** Adult recreational runners in a device-based observational cohort; novice runners in a randomized graded-training study
- **Domain:** Sparse history; Eligibility; Progression
- **Limitations:**
  - Neither source directly validates self-report admission, a 28-day lookback, a session count, or the proposed bridge.
  - The 5205-person cohort was device-selected and only 22% female; associations do not establish causality or safety below a threshold.
  - The novice trial did not show injury prevention from the 10% rule; this does not contradict a session-distance association in a different population.

### `trail-program.strength-support-with-protocol-limits` — moderate

Resistance training improves muscle function in healthy adults; selected runner programmes improve running economy or performance over multiweek interventions. A low-dose introduction is a plausible support module, without a two-week efficacy or injury-prevention promise.

- **Sources:** `currier-2026-resistance-position-stand`, `llanos-lagos-2024`, `blagrove-2018-running-strength`, `toresdahl-2020`
- **Population:** Healthy adults, predominantly inexperienced strength trainees; runners in source-specific interventions; first-time marathon runners
- **Domain:** Strength inclusion; First finish
- **Limitations:**
  - ACSM eligibility spans 6–52 weeks; the proposed introductory actions and doses have not been tested as a complete programme.
  - Runner protocols often involve heavier resistance or plyometrics; results do not validate a low-load home template.
  - One 720-person first-marathon trial found no reduction in overuse injuries causing noncompletion; no personal protective guarantee is allowed.

### `trail-program.eccentric-control-not-descent-equivalence` — low

Eccentric and isometric preconditioning can attenuate some subsequent laboratory downhill soreness or strength-loss outcomes. This supports evaluating controlled lower-limb strength as a distinct module, while withholding equivalence to outdoor descent practice or proven race transfer.

- **Sources:** `bontemps-2020-downhill-review`, `eston-1996-eccentric-preconditioning`, `lima-2018-isometric-preconditioning`, `bontemps-2025-downhill-fatigue`, `tallis-2024-downhill-female`
- **Population:** Small adult laboratory samples; predominantly young men, with one ten-woman downhill study
- **Domain:** Downhill-related strength; Eccentric control; Descent limits
- **Limitations:**
  - Eston used maximal isokinetic knee extension in ten men; Lima used maximal isometric leg press in thirty untrained men. Neither tests the proposed squat/calf/bridge programme.
  - Lima did not find protection of running economy; it is an isometric study, not an eccentric strength trial.
  - Reduced soreness after repeated downhill bouts did not imply reduced neuromuscular fatigue in Bontemps 2025.
  - No validated initial outdoor descent dose or progression law was identified in this bounded search.

### `trail-program.home-access-not-equivalent-load` — moderate

Home-based and elastic resistance training can improve strength; a gym location is not a physiological prerequisite. Equipment, available resistance and exercise skill remain material, and an arbitrary bodyweight sequence is not proven equivalent to machine or high-load training.

- **Sources:** `currier-2026-resistance-position-stand`, `lopes-2019-elastic-resistance`
- **Population:** Healthy adults in ACSM synthesis; mixed populations in eight elastic-resistance comparison studies
- **Domain:** No gym; Equipment substitution
- **Limitations:**
  - ACSM explicitly states bodyweight-supported exercise was not evaluated as a standalone intervention except Nordic hamstring curl; the exact all-bodyweight template therefore remains a guardrail.
  - Elastic review had heterogeneous protocols, load standardization gaps and low-quality studies; similar pooled strength changes do not prove interchangeability of every exercise.
  - No maximal-strength or running-performance equivalence is claimed for the proposed no-equipment module.

### `trail-program.stairs-and-incline-are-distinct` — low

Stair ascent can provide an aerobic stimulus and controlled positive-incline treadmill work provides a practical running or walking context. Descending stairs is eccentric exposure; low cardiovascular effort does not erase its muscle loading.

- **Sources:** `allison-2017-stair-climbing`, `paschalis-2013-stair-descending`, `van-hooren-2020-treadmill-biomechanics`, `bontemps-2020-downhill-review`
- **Population:** Sedentary women in small intense stair trials; twenty healthy men using a specialized escalator; adults in treadmill comparisons
- **Domain:** Stairs; Treadmill; Ascent/descent separation
- **Limitations:**
  - The intense stair study does not validate the easy introductory candidate or a trail-performance benefit. Its indexed erratum was found; no quantitative efficacy claim is extracted.
  - Paschalis used a purpose-built automatic escalator; do not copy its dose into ordinary stairs.
  - A positive-incline module cannot count as outdoor ascent observation, technical terrain or downhill adaptation. Negative-incline prescription remains separately unresolved.

### `trail-program.dual-sessions-are-feasible-not-universal-spacing` — moderate

Concurrent aerobic and strength programmes can improve both capacities. Session proximity may matter, particularly for explosive strength; separating sessions is a reasonable scheduling option, but no single interval guarantees recovery or superiority.

- **Sources:** `schumann-2022-concurrent-training`, `concurrent-2026-umbrella`, `blagrove-2018-running-strength`
- **Population:** Healthy adult concurrent-training participants, mainly recreationally trained; runner-specific programmes
- **Domain:** Dual sessions; Joint budget
- **Limitations:**
  - The 2022 meta-analysis included 43 supervised studies; explosive-strength attenuation was more evident within the same session, with at-least-three-hour subgroup separation. This is not a validated safety cutoff.
  - The 2026 umbrella review found no significant sequence effect overall and limited highly trained evidence.
  - Four-hour candidate separation and 48/72-hour buffers are Praxys guardrails, not biological laws.
  - Running time and strength time share calendar capacity but are not physiologically interchangeable.

### `trail-program.run-walk-is-a-practice-option` — low

A run/walk strategy may reduce perceived discomfort while preserving similar completion times in a small non-elite road-marathon trial. It can be offered as a practice format; it does not establish reduced cardiac stress, an optimal ratio or first-trail completion readiness.

- **Sources:** `hottenrott-2016-run-walk`, `de-waal-2021-performance-review`
- **Population:** Forty-two non-elite road-marathon participants; indirect transfer to adult trail novices with a running base
- **Domain:** First finish; Run/walk; Hiking
- **Limitations:**
  - Only indexed abstract was read for the run/walk trial; exact study pacing intervals are not reconstructed.
  - No direct validation of the proposed 4:1/2:1/1:1 introductory ratios or a first-50-km schedule.
  - Walking on technical or steep terrain still has distinct demands; comfortable effort does not establish safe footing.

### `trail-program.fifty-km-needs-duration-specific-coverage` — low

Single-stage ultramarathon preparation requires attention to duration, terrain, intake tolerance and environmental context. A 50,000-m goal belongs within the requested product scope but cannot be covered by relabelling a 49,999-m non-ultra contract or by distance alone.

- **Sources:** `costa-2019-ultramarathon-nutrition`, `tiller-2019-ultra-nutrition`, `bontemps-2020-downhill-review`
- **Population:** Adults described in single-stage ultramarathon nutrition syntheses and downhill research
- **Domain:** 50 km inclusive; Long-duration preparation
- **Limitations:**
  - The sources do not validate a complete first-50-km programme, longest-run prescription, back-to-back dose, vertical progression or personal finish probability.
  - Ultra nutrition literature spans distances and durations beyond 50 km; transport to a particular course must remain explicit.
  - General support modules can be prescribed as candidates; unresolved long-duration and course-specific coverage prevents a full race-preparation claim.

### `trail-program.fuel-practice-is-individual` — moderate

Practising a tolerable nutrition strategy during prolonged training is evidence-consistent. Published carbohydrate guidance varies with context; a starter practice target must not be advertised as a complete personal race nutrition plan.

- **Sources:** `tiller-2019-ultra-nutrition`, `costa-2019-ultramarathon-nutrition`, `jeukendrup-2017-gut-training`
- **Population:** Adult endurance and ultramarathon runners represented in guidance; some gut-mechanism evidence is animal-based
- **Domain:** Fuel practice; Long-duration support
- **Limitations:**
  - ISSN reports 30–50 g/h carbohydrate for single-stage ultra racing as category C guidance; Costa discusses approximately 90 g/h for prolonged exercise while explicitly questioning ultra tolerability.
  - The 2017 gut review relies partly on animal mechanistic extrapolation; no promise of adaptation after fourteen days.
  - Exact starter timing and progression are candidate guardrails; no fixed fluid or sodium dose, restrictive diet, supplement or clinical GI treatment is inferred.
  - Thirst-based practical advice is not assurance against heat illness or a rehydration prescription between closely spaced sessions.

### `trail-program.taper-direction-transfers-with-limits` — moderate

Reducing training volume before competition while retaining familiar intensity and frequency can improve endurance time-trial performance in studied athletes. This supports a reviewable taper option, with explicit uncertainty for first-time trail and 50-km goals.

- **Sources:** `wang-2023`
- **Population:** Well-trained endurance athletes in fourteen studies, predominantly male
- **Domain:** Taper; Preparation cycle
- **Limitations:**
  - The review required VO2max above 55 ml/kg/min or at least well-trained classification; recreational first-finish applicability is indirect.
  - The review reports effective 41–60% volume reduction and durations up to 21 days; exact fourteen-day 60%/40% remaining volumes are a Praxys choice.
  - No overload before taper is prescribed; study-level overload effects do not authorize novel workload.
  - Running volume evidence does not quantify trail vertical or strength taper dose; those stay distinct guardrails or unavailable modules.

### `trail-program.feedback-does-not-measure-adaptation` — low

Load changes and non-medical interruptions warrant reassessment. Fitness outcomes change at different rates, and symptom feedback is useful for adjustment without quantifying adaptation, recovered strength or personal injury risk.

- **Sources:** `frandsen-2025`, `buist-2008`, `barbieri-2023`, `bontemps-2025-downhill-fatigue`
- **Population:** Adult recreational runners and heterogeneous trained endurance populations
- **Domain:** Progression; Regression; Restart; Review
- **Limitations:**
  - No reviewed source validates exact 7/14-day review cadence, completion fractions, five-minute increments, percentage restart doses or a safe ACWR zone.
  - The candidate uses no fixed fitness-loss estimate and no automated return after illness/injury.
  - Absence of soreness is insufficient for descent progression or race readiness.

## Known gaps

- Validated first-50-km admission, longest-run/back-to-back/vertical progression and full-course workload prescription.
- Outdoor descent initiation without prior comparable exposure, negative-incline treadmill and technical-ground progression.
- Direct comparative effectiveness of the proposed home bodyweight template, reported-history bridge and exact run/walk ratios.
- Individual fuel/hydration needs, heat/altitude exposure and complete event-day nutrition policy.
- Performance-specific quality progression and an integrated automated full-cycle trial across all intents.

## Conflicting findings

- Strength literature supports selected adaptations but a first-marathon programme did not prevent noncompletion injuries.
- Reduced downhill soreness does not necessarily reduce neuromuscular fatigue or improve running economy.
- Session-distance injury association and absence of preventive effect of a weekly 10% rule concern different exposures; neither yields a safe threshold.
- Ultra carbohydrate guidance ranges differ by exercise duration, intensity and tolerance; high intake is not automatically tolerated or required.
- Concurrent-training reviews differ by outcome and subgroup; an overall absence of sequence effect does not make every same-day arrangement equivalent.

## Follow-up questions

- Which supported module combinations can a representative no-gym/sparse-history population execute and understand without mistaking support for race readiness?
- What externally reviewed long-duration and course-specific doses can complete the 50-km policy without importing non-ultra approvals?
