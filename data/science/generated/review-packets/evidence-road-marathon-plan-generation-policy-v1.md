## Question and product purpose

For adults aged 18 years or older with current direct outdoor road-marathon capability, stable recent running history that anchors their own exposure, within-recent load, performance intent, optional target time or date, and confirmed event context, what evidence supports and limits a suggestion-only modular road-marathon performance policy covering readiness, history and load, long-run durability, intensity and race-specific work, fueling and hydration practice, taper and recovery, environment and altitude, and reassessment and outcomes?

Define the evidence boundary for one currently-capable, history-rich adult outdoor road-marathon performance policy without inheriting any 5 km, 10 km, or half-marathon numeric rule. Separate direct marathon observations and bounded source findings from Praxys guardrails. ## Scope

## Population

- Adults aged 18 years or older
- Runners with current direct outdoor road-marathon capability
- Runners with stable recent history sufficient to anchor their own exposure
- Runners whose current load remains within their recent observed pattern
- Outdoor road-marathon performance intent with optional target time or date
- Runners with athlete-confirmed event context

## Intervention or exposure

- Same-task marathon results, prediction inputs, pacing, and capability confirmation
- Weekly running volume, frequency, longest-run exposure, and durability
- Mostly-low-intensity organization, race-specific work, and training-intensity distribution
- Pre-race volume reduction, taper organization, and post-marathon reassessment
- Carbohydrate availability, prior-practice or gut-training, fluid, and sodium context
- Heat, humidity, wind, solar load, altitude, and adaptation context
- Suggestion-only modular planning and athlete-confirmed adoption

## Comparator

- Direct same-task history versus prediction, shorter-distance conversion, laboratory markers, or passive segments
- Lower versus higher observed weekly volume, frequency, or longest-run exposure
- Pyramidal, polarized, threshold-heavy, or other intensity organizations
- Disciplined versus minimal pre-race volume reduction
- Practiced versus unpracticed fueling and gastrointestinal exposure
- Under-replacement, individualized replacement, and overdrinking
- Sea-level versus acute altitude exposure or post-hypoxic adaptation

- Marathon finish time, race speed, pacing, and prediction error
- Running-economy durability and fatigue resistance
- Training organization, taper association, and uncertainty
- Endurance performance and gastrointestinal tolerance

## Claims

### `road-marathon.task-specific-capability-baseline-multifactorial` — low

Road-marathon capability and performance are multifactorial. Recent athlete-confirmed same-task history is the most direct available baseline for this policy, while anthropometric, training, physiological, pacing, and course-specific models retain unexplained variation and do not establish one universal qualification threshold.

- **Sources:** `nikolaidis-2021-athens-marathon`, `schmid-2012-female-marathon`, `salinero-2017-marathon-prediction`, `swain-2020-marathon-pacing`
- **Population:** Adult road-marathon runners; Male single-course and small female recreational cohorts represented by the direct models
- **Domain:** Capability confirmation; Direct baseline hierarchy; Performance prediction
- **Limitations:**
  - Prediction models do not establish causal training prescriptions or eligibility cutoffs.
  - The Athens model is male, single-course, and internally split rather than externally generalizable.
  - The female model includes 29 runners and does not establish a general threshold.
  - Same-task history can still be stale, contextually different, or incomplete.
- **Verified effect estimates:**
  - Athens race-speed model explained variance: 0.61 R-squared (Male runners from one course with internal data splitting)
  - Athens race-speed model standard error: 1.19 kilometres_per_hour (Male runners from one course with internal data splitting)
  - Female recreational-runner model explained variance: 0.5 R-squared (Twenty-nine recreational female marathon runners)

### `road-marathon.volume-frequency-longest-run-associative` — moderate

Weekly volume, training frequency, and longest-run exposure are associated with marathon performance and pacing, but the reviewed observational evidence does not validate exact individual weekly volume, frequency, longest-run, progression, or safety prescriptions.

- **Sources:** `fokkema-2020`, `swain-2020-marathon-pacing`
- **Population:** Adult recreational marathon entrants; Male marathoners represented by the pacing study
- **Domain:** Weekly volume; Training frequency; Longest-run exposure; Pacing
- **Limitations:**
  - Training exposure was observational and partly self-reported.
  - Categories do not establish causality, optimality, progression, or injury safety.
  - A longest run above 35 kilometres was not significantly better than 30 to 35 kilometres in the reviewed cohort.
  - Associations cannot be converted into entry gates or individual dose.
- **Verified effect estimates:**
  - Finish-time coefficient below 40 kilometres per week: 6.33 (range 0.18 to 12.48) minutes (Adjusted observational comparison with 40 to 65 kilometres per week)
  - Finish-time coefficient above 65 kilometres per week: -14.09 (range -22.47 to -5.72) minutes (Adjusted observational comparison with 40 to 65 kilometres per week)
  - Finish-time coefficient for longest run below 25 kilometres: 13.44 (range 5.34 to 21.55) minutes (Adjusted observational marathon model)

### `road-marathon.durability-relevant-no-field-cutoff` — very_low

Fatigue resistance and running-economy durability are relevant marathon concepts, and one small cross-sectional male study associated regular long runs and higher volume with less running-economy deterioration. The evidence does not validate a standardized product-ready field, protocol, cutoff, or mandatory long-run rule.

- **Sources:** `zanini-2026-durability`
- **Population:** Performance-matched well-trained male runners similar to the study cohort
- **Domain:** Durability; Running economy; Long-run context
- **Limitations:**
  - Cross-sectional design cannot establish that changing volume or long runs causes improved durability.
  - The sample included 26 men and does not validate subgroup transfer.
  - The abstract does not provide a product-ready field-test qualification rule.
- **Verified effect estimates:**
  - Running-economy deterioration in the better-durability group: 3.1 percent (Cross-sectional comparison in 26 performance-matched well-trained men)
  - Running-economy deterioration in the lower-durability group: 6.0 percent (Cross-sectional comparison in 26 performance-matched well-trained men)
  - Longest-run association with running-economy deterioration: -0.67 correlation_coefficient (Cross-sectional association in 26 well-trained men)
  - Training-volume association with running-economy deterioration: -0.48 correlation_coefficient (Cross-sectional association in 26 well-trained men)

### `road-marathon.marathon-tid-mostly-low-observational` — low

Direct marathon training-intensity-distribution data describe greater zone-one volume among faster runners and pyramidal organization in more than 80 percent of the fastest group across 151,813 marathons from 119,452 runners. The observational, zone-definition-dependent analysis does not establish an individual low-intensity percentage, session count, spacing rule, race-specific dose, or causal optimum.

- **Sources:** `muniz-pumares-2025-marathon-tid`
- **Population:** Marathon runners represented in the large training-data analysis
- **Domain:** Training-intensity distribution; Mostly-low organization; Performance-level association
- **Limitations:**
  - Observational training data cannot establish causal superiority.
  - Zone definitions and data quality affect classification.
  - Performance groups are evidence cohorts rather than permanent runner identities.
  - Abstract-bounded extraction cannot support exact schedule parameters.

### `road-marathon.taper-support-exact-parameters-uncertain` — moderate

Disciplined pre-race volume reduction is associated with better marathon performance, while mixed-endurance taper evidence more strongly supports reduced volume with maintained intensity and frequency. Exact marathon taper window, reduction, intensity exposure, frequency, accounting, and personal benefit remain uncertain.

- **Sources:** `smyth-lawlor-2021`, `hug-2014-marathon-taper`, `wang-2023`
- **Population:** Recreational marathon runners in the observational study; Endurance athletes in the broader meta-analysis
- **Domain:** Taper; Pre-race volume reduction; Event preparation
- **Limitations:**
  - The marathon taper study is observational and cannot prove a fixed benefit.
  - Mixed sports and distances make the meta-analysis indirect to road-marathon automation.
  - Autonomic findings do not select a performance taper template.
  - No source validates one personal taper percentage or guaranteed gain.
- **Verified effect estimates:**
  - Median strict three-week taper association: 332.4 seconds (Observational marathon comparison with minimal taper)
  - Median strict three-week taper association: 2.6 percent (Observational marathon comparison with minimal taper)
  - Time-trial performance after taper: -0.45 (range -0.68 to -0.23) standardized_mean_difference (Mixed-endurance meta-analysis; 95 percent confidence interval)
  - Performance with 41 to 60 percent volume reduction: -0.77 (range -1.23 to -0.3) standardized_mean_difference (Mixed-endurance taper subgroup; 95 percent confidence interval)
  - Performance with 8 to 14 day taper: -1.47 (range -2.75 to -0.19) standardized_mean_difference (Mixed-endurance taper-duration subgroup; 95 percent confidence interval)

### `road-marathon.pacing-prediction-retains-individual-error` — low

Marathon pacing and prediction models retain material individual error. Raw-training critical-speed estimation did not prove that source sessions were maximal, and model output cannot create a personal success probability or justify target-gap dose escalation.

- **Sources:** `smyth-muniz-pumares-2020`, `nikolaidis-2021-athens-marathon`, `schmid-2012-female-marathon`, `salinero-2017-marathon-prediction`, `swain-2020-marathon-pacing`
- **Population:** Recreational and amateur marathon runners represented by the prediction studies
- **Domain:** Prediction error; Pacing; Target communication
- **Limitations:**
  - Models use different populations, courses, inputs, and validation approaches.
  - Raw sessions were not proven maximal efforts.
  - Group error does not calibrate one athlete's success probability.
  - Prediction does not establish readiness, safety, or a training dose.
- **Verified effect estimates:**
  - Best raw-training critical-speed marathon prediction error: 7.67 percent (Recreational marathon runners)
  - Approximate error around the cohort average finish time: 18.0 minutes (Approximately 18 minutes around an average 230-minute marathon)

### `road-marathon.carbohydrate-support-contextual` — moderate

Acute carbohydrate feeding supports prolonged endurance performance at population level, but loading and during-exercise intake depend on event duration, practiced tolerance, feeding opportunity, environment, and athlete context. The marathon distance label alone does not validate an exact personal loading or intake rule.

- **Sources:** `ramos-campo-2024`, `burke-2011`, `podlogar-2022`
- **Population:** Adult endurance athletes represented by the systematic review and sports-nutrition literature
- **Domain:** Carbohydrate availability; Endurance performance; Fueling context
- **Limitations:**
  - Protocols, exercise duration, carbohydrate form, and baseline nutrition vary.
  - Broader endurance evidence is not a marathon-only prescription.
  - Population ergogenic effects do not establish an individual minimum, maximum, or guarantee.

### `road-marathon.gut-training-tolerance-not-universal` — low

Repeated feeding practice or gut-training may reduce gastrointestinal discomfort and carbohydrate malabsorption in some endurance athletes, but protocols are heterogeneous and no universal schedule, dose, guaranteed adaptation, or race-day tolerance exists.

- **Sources:** `martinez-2023`, `podlogar-2022`
- **Population:** Adult endurance athletes represented by the reviewed feeding-practice studies
- **Domain:** Fueling practice; Gastrointestinal tolerance; Gut training
- **Limitations:**
  - Few heterogeneous protocols support the numeric findings.
  - Tolerance improvement is not guaranteed and may not transfer to race context.
  - No source selects one automated marathon gut-training protocol.
- **Verified effect estimates:**
  - Gastrointestinal discomfort reduction: 47.0 percent (Two repeated-carbohydrate-feeding protocols summarized in Martinez et al. 2023)
  - Carbohydrate malabsorption reduction: 45.0 to 54.0 percent (Two gut-training studies summarized in Martinez et al. 2023)

### `road-marathon.fluid-sodium-needs-variable` — moderate

Fluid and sodium needs vary within and between athletes and contexts. Both inadequate replacement and overdrinking matter, including exercise-associated hyponatremia risk. No distance-only millilitres-per- hour, sodium, body-mass-loss, or replacement rule is safe or validated for individualized marathon automation.

- **Sources:** `mcdermott-2017-fluid`, `hew-butler-2015-eah`, `baker-2017-sweat`
- **Population:** Physically active adults and endurance athletes covered by the position statement, consensus, and review
- **Domain:** Fluid replacement; Sodium; Sweat variability; Exercise-associated hyponatremia
- **Limitations:**
  - Position and consensus statements bound safety but do not validate one product prescription.
  - Sweat rate and sodium concentration vary by method, athlete, environment, and acclimation.
  - Medical diagnosis and treatment remain outside this performance policy.

### `road-marathon.altitude-capacity-no-personal-correction` — moderate

Acute altitude reduces aerobic capacity and fixed-speed endurance in controlled conditions, while hypoxic adaptation evidence does not validate an individualized marathon altitude correction or acclimation schedule. Chamber findings must not be converted into a personal race time or pace adjustment.

- **Sources:** `wehrlin-2006-altitude`, `bonetti-hopkins-2009`
- **Population:** Endurance athletes represented by the acute chamber study and hypoxia meta-analysis
- **Domain:** Altitude; Acute exercise capacity; Acclimation and adaptation limits
- **Limitations:**
  - Fixed-speed time to exhaustion is not self-paced marathon performance.
  - Acute chamber exposure does not define race-course altitude effects.
  - The hypoxia meta-analysis does not select an individual marathon acclimation schedule.
- **Verified effect estimates:**
  - Acute maximum-oxygen-uptake change per 1000 metres: -6.3 percent (Acute hypobaric-chamber exposure in endurance athletes)
  - Fixed-speed time-to-exhaustion change per 1000 metres: -14.5 percent (Acute hypobaric-chamber exposure in endurance athletes)

### `road-marathon.recovery-subgroup-outcome-rules-unvalidated` — very_low

Post-marathon recovery, subgroup-specific dose changes, race-density thresholds, and fixed outcome windows remain unvalidated for automation. Renal recovery findings after a marathon concern one physiological domain and do not establish general readiness, workout spacing, or medical clearance.

- **Sources:** `hernando-2022-marathon-recovery`, `schmid-2012-female-marathon`, `fokkema-2020`
- **Population:** Amateur marathon runners represented by the renal recovery study; Female and mixed recreational cohorts represented by the prediction and training studies
- **Domain:** Post-marathon recovery; Subgroup applicability; Race density; Outcome interpretation
- **Limitations:**
  - Renal recovery is not a general readiness measure.
  - Prediction and observational cohort differences do not establish subgroup dose modifiers.
  - No reviewed source validates one race-priority threshold, reassessment cadence, or meaningful-change window.

## Known gaps

- A prospectively evaluated deterministic adult road-marathon performance policy
- A validated direct-result qualification algorithm and freshness window
- A validated minimum stable-history count or current-load qualification algorithm
- Randomized marathon-specific weekly volume, frequency, progression, longest-run, or durability-field cutoffs
- Causal marathon-specific training-intensity distribution, quality-session, spacing, race-specific, or workout-template rules
- A validated marathon taper window, reduction, accounting method, short-horizon alternative, or personal gain
- A validated individual carbohydrate-loading, intake, gut-training, fluid, or sodium policy
- A validated individual marathon environmental or altitude correction and acclimation schedule
- General readiness and spacing rules after a marathon rather than one-domain recovery observations
- Race-density, event-priority, subgroup-dose, outcome-window, meaningful-change, and rollout thresholds

## Conflicting findings

- Higher observed volume and longer runs associate with performance without establishing causal dose, safety, or an overdistance requirement.
- Durability is marathon-relevant, while no standardized field protocol or cutoff is ready for automation.
- Faster runners commonly show pyramidal organization, while observational and zone-definition limits prevent one exact distribution.
- Marathon observational taper findings and mixed-endurance meta-analysis support disciplined volume reduction without selecting one exact marathon template.
- Carbohydrate can improve endurance performance, while practical benefit and tolerance vary and distance alone cannot choose intake.
- Both inadequate replacement and overdrinking matter, making a universal distance-only fluid or sodium rule unsafe.
- Acute altitude effects are material, while chamber coefficients do not define an individual marathon correction.

## Follow-up questions

- Which direct road-marathon result types, metadata, freshness, and qualification algorithm should count as current capability?
- How much stable recent history is sufficient to anchor frequency, volume, long-run, quality density, and load?
- Should any exact plan length, frequency, progression, long-run, durability, intensity, race-specific, or workout rule be selected?
- Which taper, event-minute accounting, recovery, and reassessment choices should be reviewed?
- Which fueling, loading, gut-training, fluid, sodium, and prompt rules can be implemented without distance-only automation?
- Which environmental inputs are required, and can any altitude or acclimation behavior be justified?
- How should no-event rolling preparation route without inventing a maximal marathon simulation?
- Which race-density, subgroup, outcome, pilot, implementation, and activation thresholds require separate decisions?
