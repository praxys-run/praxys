## Question and product purpose

For nonclinical adults with sufficient recent general running but variable trail, descent and strength experience, what supports bounded strength and treadmill modules in a fourteen-day resource-aware base proposal, and what prevents claiming terrain equivalence, descent readiness, safe progression or short-block efficacy?

Support a reviewable v3 choice among three resource presets and a useful basic proposal with explicit specialty gaps. This review does not approve any dose, replace accepted history, activate a contract, or authorize production use.

## Scope

## Population

- Adults aged 18 or older, nonclinical, with stable recent running
- Strength novices and familiar trainees considered separately
- Frequent-trail, city-with-occasional-trail, and road-plus-gym access contexts

## Intervention or exposure

- Low-volume strength introduction or bounded familiar training
- Level/positive-incline treadmill running
- Outdoor trail exposure where observed history supports it

## Comparator

- Running-only
- Other resistance prescriptions
- Level versus graded and treadmill versus overground running

## Outcomes

- Muscle function and running performance
- Fatigue/soreness and applicability
- Limits of dose and transfer claims

## Inclusion criteria

- Prefer a current authoritative synthesis for healthy-adult strength plus direct runner evidence.
- Use direct treadmill/graded comparisons for modality limits, and contrasting downhill studies for soreness/fatigue interpretation.
- Peer-reviewed adult human studies/reviews with verified stable metadata; strong conclusions bounded to relevant accessible text.

## Exclusion criteria

- Clinical/rehabilitation, pediatric, animal, supplement and preprint evidence used as direct training-policy support.
- Acute or population-average effects converted into a personal guarantee, universal transfer ratio or safe dose.
- Inaccessible methods or full text presented as read.

## Claims

### `trail-resource.strength-support-not-short-block-promise` — moderate

Resistance training can improve muscle function in healthy adults, including inexperienced trainees. Some runner strength protocols improve running performance, but these findings do not validate a fourteen-day trail plan or guarantee a personal performance gain.

- **Sources:** `currier-2026-resistance-position-stand`, `llanos-lagos-2024`
- **Population:** Healthy adults; trained middle- and long-distance runners represented in the reviews
- **Domain:** Strength inclusion; Claim limits
- **Limitations:**
  - ACSM included interventions lasting 6–52 weeks, predominantly inexperienced trainees; runner review interventions lasted 6–40 weeks.
  - Runner review certainty ranged from very low to moderate; high-load and combined effects do not establish efficacy of this low-load introductory template.
  - The runner review included participants over 16; Praxys remains adult-only.

### `trail-resource.introductory-dose-is-a-guardrail` — moderate

A low-volume introduction and avoiding repetition failure are evidence-consistent options. Published studies and guidance permit multiple useful prescriptions; they do not establish exact personal starting sets, repetitions, load, rest or repetitions-in-reserve thresholds.

- **Sources:** `currier-2026-resistance-position-stand`, `blagrove-2018-running-strength`, `stohanzl-2018-minimal-strength`
- **Population:** Healthy resistance-training novices and experienced adults; Recreational runners in source-specific protocols
- **Domain:** Introductory strength; Frequency and load selection
- **Limitations:**
  - ACSM describes benefits with a single set and greater gains with additional sets; its primary recommendation is at least twice weekly, not proof of one optimum.
  - Blagrove reports studies often starting at 1–2 sets, typical 2–3 minute rests, and predominantly 2–3 weekly sessions. These are study descriptions/reviewer recommendations.
  - The 41-woman 10-week trial compared one or two 30-minute strength sessions with running-only while reallocating a fixed three-hour weekly budget; abstract reports improvement within concurrent groups, not proof of superiority or a fourteen-day effect.
  - ACSM discusses 2–3 RIR but explicitly notes insufficient evidence to quantify exact RIR targets; Praxys targets remain guardrails.

### `trail-resource.strength-does-not-guarantee-injury-prevention` — moderate

One self-directed strength programme in first-time marathon runners did not significantly reduce overuse injuries causing noncompletion or improve mean finishing time; strength inclusion cannot be presented as an injury-prevention guarantee.

- **Sources:** `toresdahl-2020`
- **Population:** 720 initially randomized adult first-time marathon runners
- **Domain:** Injury claim limits
- **Limitations:**
  - One 12-week, three-times-weekly programme; observation participants could also perform strength training.
  - Self-reported adherence/injury and no direct technique observation limit interpretation; this does not establish that all strength training lacks benefit.

### `trail-resource.treadmill-is-context-not-terrain-equivalence` — moderate

Motorized treadmill running provides a running training context with many biomechanical similarities to overground running, alongside meaningful differences. It does not establish equivalent outdoor trail ascent, descent, footing or performance exposure.

- **Sources:** `van-hooren-2020-treadmill-biomechanics`, `miller-2019-treadmill-physiology`
- **Population:** Healthy adults in acute treadmill/overground crossover studies
- **Domain:** Indoor running; Transfer and provenance
- **Limitations:**
  - Biomechanics review covered non-inclined, quasi-constant-speed motorized running, not technical trails; outcome certainty was mostly low/very low.
  - Physiological/perceptual differences can depend on speed; no universal 1% incline correction is established for this policy.
  - These are acute comparisons, not longitudinal evidence for replacing outdoor trail training.

### `trail-resource.uphill-downhill-specific-and-dose-uncertain` — low

Uphill and downhill running impose different mechanical and physiological demands. Uphill training is a plausible optional module, but heterogeneous training evidence does not choose a universal gradient, session dose or superiority over conventional training.

- **Sources:** `lu-2025-graded-running`, `tham-2026-uphill-training`
- **Population:** Healthy athletes and recreational adults represented by graded-running studies
- **Domain:** Graded training; Separate ascent/descent
- **Limitations:**
  - Lu is a narrative synthesis; inaccessible and non-English evidence was excluded and no formal risk-of-bias synthesis is reported.
  - Tham included 13 small trials, predominantly athletes/males; only seven entered quantitative analyses and pooled outcomes did not show significant superiority for strength, jumping or aerobic capacity.
  - Many interventions were high-intensity sprints at substantially steeper grades, not the proposed easy 2% familiarization template.
  - The proposed gradient, duration and frequency are Praxys guardrails, not validated safe or effective individual doses.

### `trail-resource.downhill-adaptation-not-clearance` — low

Repeated downhill exposure may attenuate soreness in source-specific protocols, but soreness and neuromuscular fatigue can adapt differently. Running fitness, strength work or absence of soreness cannot establish descent readiness or an initial safe downhill dose.

- **Sources:** `bontemps-2025-downhill-fatigue`, `tallis-2024-downhill-female`
- **Population:** 12 healthy recreationally active adults without prior downhill conditioning; 10 trained female distance runners naive to prolonged downhill running
- **Domain:** Descent exposure; Feedback limits
- **Limitations:**
  - Small supervised laboratory studies with specific grades, speeds and prior familiarization.
  - Bontemps found reduced quadriceps soreness after ten sessions without attenuation of neuromuscular fatigue to a standardized bout; Tallis did not measure neuromuscular recovery directly.
  - Neither study validates outdoor descent dose, a 42/56-day readiness cutoff, a monthly exposure budget or equivalence with gym training.

### `trail-resource.concurrent-scheduling-requires-context` — low

Running and strength can be combined within a programme. Acute fatigue and available time should be considered, but the reviewed evidence does not establish a universal recovery interval or a minutes-to-minutes physiological load equivalence.

- **Sources:** `blagrove-2018-running-strength`, `currier-2026-resistance-position-stand`, `stohanzl-2018-minimal-strength`
- **Population:** Adult runners and healthy adults in source-specific concurrent/strength programmes
- **Domain:** Joint scheduling; Recovery and time accounting
- **Limitations:**
  - Blagrove discusses separate days, more than three hours within-day, and at least 24 hours before intensive running as practical recommendations, not individual safety laws.
  - A fixed available-time budget is a scheduling constraint; reallocating time does not prove equal physiological exposure.
  - The current single-session-per-day candidate limit is a software scope decision, not a biological rule.

### `trail-resource.subjective-effort-is-not-a-universal-zone` — low

Comfortable speech and perceived effort can support practical aerobic intensity adjustment, but exact subjective thresholds and strength RIR values cannot be treated as universal physiological or safety thresholds.

- **Sources:** `bok-2022-subjective-intensity`, `currier-2026-resistance-position-stand`
- **Population:** Healthy/active adults represented by subjective-intensity literature; healthy adults in resistance training syntheses
- **Domain:** Effort cues; No sole safety controller
- **Limitations:**
  - Talk-test review was verified at indexed abstract level only; no individual threshold or test equivalence is asserted.
  - Speed, gradient, symptoms, form and mode remain relevant; comfortable speech does not measure downhill mechanical load.

### `trail-resource.resource-frequency-does-not-prove-capacity` — low

Resource availability and training exposure are different constructs. Sparse records or zero calendar-week aggregates cannot establish zero biological capacity, and resource calendars do not themselves prove comparable terrain exposure.

- **Sources:** `lu-2025-graded-running`, `bontemps-2025-downhill-fatigue`, `van-hooren-2020-treadmill-biomechanics`
- **Population:** Adults in the reviewed terrain-specific studies; indirect applicability to resource-aware planning
- **Domain:** Missingness; History interpretation
- **Limitations:**
  - No reviewed source prospectively validates the proposed resource presets, exposure-count thresholds, calendar budgets or automated plan generator.
  - Unknown, no recorded exposure, athlete statements and measured exposure must retain their distinct provenance.

## Known gaps

- No validation of the full fourteen-day v3 generator or a first-block efficacy/safety guarantee.
- No validated personal introductory strength dose or precise RIR cutoff.
- No equivalence of treadmill, stairs, gym, ascent, descent or technical trail exposure.
- No supported automatic downhill initiation for absent/unmeasured descent exposure.
- No biological interpretation for a zero sparse-week median or the proposed 56/28-day budgets.
- No trail-specific taper, outdoor hiking dose, strength progression, fixed fueling or recovery guarantee.

## Conflicting findings

- Some strength protocols improve running performance while a self-directed marathon strength programme did not improve mean finish time or prevent noncompletion injuries.
- Many treadmill biomechanics are similar to overground, but important outcomes and physiological comparisons differ.
- Downhill soreness attenuation can coexist with persistent neuromuscular fatigue; uphill pooled outcomes do not prove superiority.

## Follow-up questions

- Does the owner find the structured base-plus-gym proposal feasible without interpreting it as complete race preparation?
- Do calendar-aware history budgets reduce false all-plan blocks without silently increasing exposure?
- Do session completion, adjustment reasons and symptom feedback falsify the proposed guardrails?
